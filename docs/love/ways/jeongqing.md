# Jeongqing — Accumulated-Affection-Love

## The gap

The love that isn't pretty. The bondedness that accumulates between people who cook the same kitchen for thirty years, who fight every Tuesday, who would not say *I love you* with a gun to their head, but who would also not survive the other's absence.

English has no word for this. *Affection* is too thin. *Bond* is too neutral. *Attachment* is clinical (and often pejorative). *Familiarity* is descriptive of duration, not of the felt-substance. Korean *jeong* is the only well-known term for the thick, friction-tolerant register — the love that built itself up underneath conscious caring.

## The cross-tradition roots

Korean *jeong* (정, 情) — accumulated affection from shared time and shared friction; the substance that makes long relationships feel rooted even when the parties wouldn't describe themselves as fond. Mandarin *qing* (情) — same character, broader scope (sentiment, feeling, situation). Vietnamese *tình* (love-feeling, with shared-history register). Japanese *najimi* (the familiarity that grows from repeated co-presence). Russian *rodnoy* (rooted-in-relation, shared-substance).

The convergence: East Asian traditions in particular hold a registered word for *the bondedness that accumulates*. Modern English's commodified love-vocabulary cannot easily render it because the commodified register privileges feeling-now over substance-over-time.

## Negative-space evidence

> "They fought constantly, and would not have known how to be without each other."
> "She didn't always like him, but she had loved him for so long that the loving had become a fact about who she was."
> "The bond was thick — not because they were close, but because they had accumulated each other."

The novel-sentence English needs because it has no compact word.
