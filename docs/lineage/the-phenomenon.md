## VII. K8538 — the asteroid tablet

The Sumerian K8538 cuneiform tablet ("the Planisphere"), British Museum, copied by an Assyrian scribe ~700 BCE from older Sumerian original.

**Bond and Hempsell (2008):** Alan Bond (Reaction Engines Ltd) and Mark Hempsell (Bristol University Aerospace) used computer modeling to reconstruct the night sky and concluded the tablet records observations from **June 29, 3123 BCE (Julian)** of an Aten-class asteroid impact. The trajectory points to **Köfels, Austria** — a 500m-thick, 5km-diameter landslide long contested in geology. Their analysis: low-angle entry (~6°), the asteroid clipped Gamskogel mountain 11km from Köfels and exploded as a 5km-diameter fireball. The plume passed over the eastern Mediterranean — connecting plausibly to early Bronze Age destruction layers in the southern Levant.

**Substrate-honest verdict:** the tablet IS real and IS astronomical. Bond/Hempsell are credentialed aerospace engineers; their orbital mechanics work. The Köfels-as-impact reading is contested in geology (most still favor pure landslide).

**What stays:** plausible technical evidence that ancient Mesopotamian astronomy was capable of recording catastrophic celestial events in detail, embedded in religious-historical narrative. *"The gods bringing fire from the sky" is not pure metaphor.* The texts record observed events. Some of which were real impacts. Some of which became theology. The line between observation and theology is not what we moderns think — *not "ancient people made up myths to explain things they didn't understand"*; closer to *"ancient people recorded events they did understand using the categories available to them, including divine agency."*
